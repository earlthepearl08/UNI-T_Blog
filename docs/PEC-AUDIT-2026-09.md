# PEC / PS compliance audit — UNI-T Philippines calculators

**Date:** 14 September 2026  
**Scope:** all six tools in `tools/` (10,527 lines)  
**Method:** 12 engineering dimensions checked independently, then every defect attacked by
three separate skeptics (a standards pedant, a code reader, and a practising PH engineer)
whose instructions were to *refute* it. A finding survives only if fewer than half succeeded.  
**Scale:** 145 agents, 0 failures.

## Result

| | count |
|---|---|
| Upheld after adversarial review | **31** (19 safety-critical, 8 compliance, 4 accuracy) |
| Refuted and discarded | 13 |
| Figures checked and confirmed correct | 41 |
| Lower-severity defects reported but not adversarially tested | 44 |

> **Can this be 100% certain? No.** Per-band confidence in the published ampacity/breaker figures: ~90% that 2.0-8.0 mm² rows and their 15/20/30 A caps are correct and safe for PH residential branch circuits (NEC-traceable, internally consistent across three files, corroborated in the audit). ~60-70% for 14-38 mm² (AWG-coincident, so self-consistent, but the metric-label question is unresolved). Under 40% for 50 mm² and above (single-transcription, no cross-check). Near zero that any output is code-compliant AS PRINTED, because the correction factors that would make it so are absent from all six files.

## Verdict

NOT CORRECT AS PUBLISHED, AND NOT DEMONSTRABLY PS/PEC-COMPLIANT — though the failure is narrower and more fixable than the 30-item defect list suggests.

WHAT CAN BE ASSERTED WITH HIGH CONFIDENCE (~90%)
The small end of the table is sound. The 2.0 / 3.5 / 5.5 / 8.0 mm² rows (20 / 25 / 35 / 50 A) and their 15 / 20 / 30 A breaker caps coincide exactly with NEC 310.16 75 °C copper at 14 / 12 / 10 / 8 AWG, are consistent across all three sizing tools, and are the rows that carry the overwhelming majority of this site's traffic. The conductor SERIES is right — PNS/JIS metric (2.0/3.5/5.5/8.0/14/22/30/38/50/60/80/100/125/150), not the IEC series — which is the thing most foreign-derived calculators get wrong for PH, and this site got it right deliberately. Copper is disclosed in visible text on all three sizing tools (safety-calculator.html:789, wiring-reference.html:983, power-calculator.html:1273). The pure-physics content is clean: I checked every row of the resistor colour-code table (wiring-reference.html:954-962), the Xc/Xl worked examples (:1245, :1250 — 265.3 Ω and 18.85 Ω, both correct at 60 Hz), the 3-phase power example (:1240 — 5,594 W, correct), all 17 IP levels against IEC 60529 (:1259-1281), the AWG↔mm² conversion formula (safety-calculator.html:1573-1574 — exact in both directions), and the temperature/pressure converters. All correct. The MV/HV guard (power-calculator.html:1127-1140) and the CAT-voltage-before-current clamp filter (:1153-1170) are genuinely well-engineered safety behaviour and better than most commercial tools.

WHAT IS GENUINELY UNCERTAIN
Everything from 50 mm² up. Those ampacities have no independent cross-check — they do not coincide with any AWG value, and they trace to a single Philippine website's transcription. The 325 mm² = 420 A row in power-calculator.html:1071 cannot be traced to any table anyone in this audit found. And the audit's two separate findings — "AWG labels are one size small" and "50-125 mm² ampacities understate the PEC column" — are two descriptions of one underlying fact: the table was built from an AWG source and given metric headings. Below 38 mm² that is conservative-to-correct. Above it, nobody knows, including me.

WHAT IS WRONG REGARDLESS OF HOW THE PEC QUESTION RESOLVES
The three variables that actually decide whether a conductor overheats — ambient correction, conductor bundling, and termination temperature — are computed nowhere in 10,527 lines. In a PH ceiling void at 40 °C with six current-carrying conductors in one pipe, the factors stack to roughly 0.88 × 0.80 = 0.70. A 14 mm² conductor the tool calls 65 A is then ~45 A, and safety-calculator.html:1523 will instruct the user to "protect this run with a breaker no larger than 70A". That is a fire path, and it does not depend on the PEC table dispute at all. The tools disclose this in prose (power-calculator.html:1273, safety-calculator.html:1529) and then print the undated number anyway — a caveat next to a big green number loses to the big green number every time.

And the audit itself contradicted itself on the one issue that would move every number in the 8.0-50 mm² range: the 60 °C-vs-75 °C termination column was UPHELD in the ampacity dimension (1/3 refuted) and REFUTED in the derating dimension (3/3). That is not a resolved question, and it is the highest-leverage open item in the whole review.

THE HONEST BOTTOM LINE
This is a competent, unusually self-aware piece of engineering content with a dangerous mismatch between its confidence and its evidence. The failure mode is not bad arithmetic — the arithmetic is largely right. It is that a tool which cannot see ambient temperature, raceway population, termination markings, duty cycle, motor code letter, or available fault current is printing single-value answers in the register of a specification. The gap between "preliminary basis only" in the disclaimer and "Wire: 14 mm² / Breaker: 70 A" in the result card is the entire problem.

WHAT I WOULD DO THIS WEEK, IN ORDER
1. Fix the four CAT ratings (UT89XD, UT203, UT202A, UT206A). Primary-sourced, checkable against the physical faceplate, and the findings most likely to hurt someone holding a meter.
2. Make every sizing tool refuse to print a conductor above 38 mm² — hand off to a PEE instead. This removes the entire unsourced region of the table at a stroke and costs the owner nothing, because nobody sizes a 200 mm² feeder off a blog calculator anyway.
3. Delete the AWG column from safety-calculator's wire table. It needs no source to remove and it is the finding most likely to cause a wrong purchase at the counter.
4. Stop printing the NEMA starter size (see unexamined claims — it is wrong at 2, 5 and 10 HP).
5. Resolve the 60/75 °C question with one phone call to Royu / America's / Schneider PH asking what temperature their panelboard and breaker terminals are listed at. A week of searching could not settle this; a supplier can settle it in ten minutes.
6. Buy PEC 2017 Part 1 from IIEE and pay a PEE for one afternoon against the six tables. This is cheaper than any further research and is the only thing that converts "probably right" into "signed".

Do not act on anything in the weaklySourced list below before step 6.

### [SAFETY-CRITICAL] No ambient-temperature correction is applied anywhere in any of the six tools

- **Where:** `power-calculator.html:1048`
- **Survived:** 1 of 3 skeptics refuted it
- **Source strength:** `nec-equiv`
- **Standard requires:** PEC 2017 requires allowable ampacity to be multiplied by the ambient correction factor when ambient exceeds 30°C (PEC Table 3.10.1.16 correction factors, cf. NEC Table 310.15(B)(1)). Verified factors: at 36-40°C, 0.88 (75°C column) / 0.82 (60°C column); at 41-45°C, 0.82 (75°C) / 0.71 (60°C).
- **Tool currently does:** A grep across all six files for any correction arithmetic (multiplication by 0.7x/0.8x/0.9x, or any variable named ambient/tempCorr/correctionFactor/derate) returns zero hits outside CSS. Every sizing path reads a fixed ampacity property and compares it directly to load current. The 30°C basis is stated in a code comment and in prose, but no ambient input field exists on any tool and no factor is ever applied.
- **Proposed fix:** Add an ambient-temperature selector (30 / 35 / 40 / 45 °C) defaulting to 40°C for PH ceiling and roof spaces, and multiply table ampacity by the correction factor before the comparison. Defaulting to 30°C is the wrong default for the Philippines even once the input exists.
- **Caveats:** Severity is safety-critical because the omission is silent in two of the four sizing outputs (see separate findings). Where it IS disclosed, the disclosure is prose only — the user is told to derate but given no factors to derate with, on a page whose whole purpose is to do the arithmetic for them. I verified the factor values in NEC only; I could not obtain PEC's own correction table, so I cannot rule out that PEC's metric table uses slightly different values.

### [SAFETY-CRITICAL] No adjustment factor for more than three current-carrying conductors in a raceway

- **Where:** `safety-calculator.html:1437`
- **Survived:** 0 of 3 skeptics refuted it
- **Source strength:** `nec-equiv`
- **Standard requires:** PEC 3.10.1.15(B)(3)(a) (cf. NEC 310.15(C)(1)) requires ampacity to be multiplied by 0.80 for 4-6 current-carrying conductors, 0.70 for 7-9, and 0.50 for 10-20, before comparing against load.
- **Tool currently does:** No conductor-count input exists on any tool, and no adjustment is applied. The comparison is made directly against the 3-conductor table value. A typical PH residential homerun conduit carrying three 2-wire circuits contains 6 current-carrying conductors and requires a 0.80 factor that the tool never applies.
- **Proposed fix:** Add a 'conductors in this conduit' input and apply the 0.80/0.70/0.50 adjustment. Note that correction and adjustment MULTIPLY: 6 conductors in a 40°C ceiling on the 75°C column is 0.88 x 0.80 = 0.70, a 30% reduction.
- **Caveats:** The 0.80/0.70 values are confirmed in NEC Table 310.15(C)(1); I did not obtain the PEC table. The 0.50 figure for 10-20 conductors appeared in search results but I could not confirm it as cleanly as the other two — treat it as less certain.

### [SAFETY-CRITICAL] FLA is computed from HP/PF/efficiency instead of read from the PEC full-load-current table, which is what Art. 4.30 requires — the result is an undersized conductor

- **Where:** `power-calculator.html:1522`
- **Survived:** 0 of 3 skeptics refuted it
- **Source strength:** `nec-equiv`
- **Standard requires:** PEC 2017 Art. 4.30 follows NEC 430.6(A)(1): for determining conductor ampacity, the rating of switches, and branch-circuit short-circuit and ground-fault protection, the values in the motor full-load-current tables shall be used INSTEAD OF the actual current marked on the motor nameplate. The PH sources cite PEC 2017 Table 4.30.14.4 (three-phase) — the NEC counterparts are Table 430.248 single-phase and Table 430.250 three-phase. The nameplate/calculated current is used ONLY for overload sizing (NEC 430.32 / PEC §4.30.3.2(A)). A computed FLA is not merely 'not the table value' — it is not the nameplate value either, so it is not a legitimate input to ANY Art. 430 rule.
- **Tool currently does:** calcMotor() derives FLA from HP x 746 / (k x V x PF x eta), using user-supplied PF and efficiency that both default to 0.85, then feeds that number into BOTH the 125% conductor rule (line 1537) and the 250% breaker rule (line 1533). The table is never consulted; there is no FLC table in the file at all.
- **Proposed fix:** Embed the PEC Table 4.30.14.4 / NEC 430.248+430.250 FLC values as a lookup keyed on (HP, phases, voltage) and drive BOTH the 125% conductor rule and the 250% breaker rule from the table value. Keep the HP/PF/eta formula but demote it to a clearly-labelled secondary figure ('estimated running current — NOT a code sizing input'), and refuse to emit a conductor or breaker recommendation for any (HP, voltage) pair that has no table entry.
- **Caveats:** I confirmed PEC tracks NEC numerically rather than assuming it: a PH source quotes PEC 2017 Table 4.30.14.4 as 65 A for a 50 HP 460 V three-phase motor, which is exactly the NEC Table 430.250 value. That equivalence is what lets me rate this nec-equiv rather than unverified. Caveat on magnitude: the error is NOT uniform. At 50 HP the formula happens to land within 0.3% of the table (129.6 A vs 130 A at 230 V), because PF x eta = 0.7225 is close to the table's implied value for large machines. The error is worst exactly where the residential/DIY audience lives — small motors, where real PF and efficiency are much poorer than 0.85. The formula is also always LOW relative to the table on single-phase, never high, so it fails in the unsafe direction.

### [SAFETY-CRITICAL] No overload protection is computed — the tool outputs a conductor protected only by a 250% short-circuit breaker, which is the fire path

- **Where:** `power-calculator.html:1594`
- **Survived:** 1 of 3 skeptics refuted it
- **Source strength:** `nec-equiv`
- **Standard requires:** NEC 430.32(A)(1) (PEC §4.30.3.2(A)): a separate overload device shall trip at no more than 125% of nameplate full-load current for motors with a marked service factor >= 1.15 or a marked temperature rise <= 40 C, and 115% for all other motors. This device — not the branch-circuit breaker — is what protects the motor AND the branch-circuit conductor against sustained overcurrent. It is why NEC 240.4(G) / PEC 2.40.4(G) permits a motor breaker to legitimately exceed conductor ampacity in the first place.
- **Tool currently does:** calcMotor() emits three sizing badges — conductor, inverse-time breaker, starter — and computes no overload value. The 115-125% rule appears only as one clause of running prose beneath the badges, with no number attached and no instruction that it is mandatory. The stated percentages are themselves correct.
- **Proposed fix:** Add a fourth, visually co-equal badge: 'Overload relay: trip at <= X A' where X = nameplate FLA x (SF >= 1.15 ? 1.25 : 1.15), using the existing motorSF input as the selector. State explicitly that this is sized on NAMEPLATE current (unlike the conductor and breaker, which use the table FLC) and that omitting it leaves the conductor unprotected.
- **Caveats:** This is the defect that turns finding #1 from a paperwork violation into a hazard, so I want the mechanism on record. Worked case, 3 HP single-phase 230 V at the tool's default PF/eta of 0.85/0.85: the tool prints conductor 2.0 mm2 (14 AWG, 20 A ampacity in the file's own table at line 1057) and a 35 A inverse-time breaker. The PEC/NEC table FLC for that motor is 17 A. A mechanically overloaded or single-phasing motor drawing 28-30 A sits indefinitely on a 20 A conductor and the 35 A breaker never trips. Nothing in the tool's output tells the user a device must exist to stop that. ### [SAFETY-CRITICAL] Conductor reactance and power factor are ignored entirely and never disclosed, understating voltage drop by up to 31% on large conductors

- **Where:** `safety-calculator.html:1435`
- **Survived:** 1 of 3 skeptics refuted it
- **Source strength:** `nec-equiv`
- **Standard requires:** Voltage drop on an AC circuit is governed by the effective impedance Z_eff = R*cos(phi) + X*sin(phi), not by R alone. NEC 2017 Chapter 9 Table 9 (PEC tracks NEC here) tabulates both XL and a pre-computed 'Effective Z at 0.85 PF' column precisely because reactance is NOT negligible: XL is roughly constant at 0.135-0.190 ohm/km regardless of conductor size, so as R falls with increasing size, X comes to dominate. For 300 kcmil copper in PVC conduit, Table 9 gives AC R = 0.144 ohm/km but Effective Z at 0.85 PF = 0.194 ohm/km.
- **Tool currently does:** The formula uses only the DC resistance constant, which is equivalent to hard-assuming power factor = 1.0 and X = 0. There is no power-factor input anywhere in the wire panel (grep for 'power factor|reactance|impedance' in this file returns hits only in the unrelated Ohm's Law panel at lines 1342-1343), and no user-visible caveat discloses the assumption. Comparing the tool's values against NEC Table 9 Effective Z at 0.85 PF: 2.0mm2 +17.8%, 5.5mm2 +5.9%, 22mm2 +0.3%, 60mm2 -18.7%, 125mm2 -31.0%, 150mm2 -27.9%. The tool is conservative on small wire and dangerously optimistic on large wire. Worked failure case: 400 V 3-phase, 250 A, 150 mm2, 190 m one-way, PF 0.85 -- tool computes Vd = 1.732*250*0.000140*190 = 11.52 V = 2.88% and reports PASS against the 3% setting; the Table 9 effective-Z method gives 1.732*250*0.000194*190 = 15.96 V = 3.99%, a FAIL. The tool green-lights an undersized feeder.
- **Proposed fix:** Add a Power Factor input (default 0.85 for mixed/motor loads, 1.0 for resistive) and a reactance column to wireData, then compute vDrop = vdFactor * I * L * (R*pf + X*Math.sqrt(1-pf*pf)). Populate X from NEC Ch.9 Table 9 XL (PVC conduit) column: 0.190, 0.177, 0.164, 0.171, 0.167, 0.157, 0.154, 0.148, 0.151, 0.144, 0.141, 0.138, 0.135, 0.135 ohm/km for 14 AWG through 300 kcmil respectively. At absolute minimum, if the PF model is not added, print a prominent user-visible caveat in the wire result card stating that the calculation assumes unity power factor and neglects conductor reactance, and that for conductors 60 mm2 and larger on inductive loads the true drop can be 20-30% higher than shown.
- **Caveats:** Table 9 values I used are from a verified text extraction of the NEC Chapter 9 Table 9 PDF (the reprint is labeled 2007 California Electrical Code but Table 9 is unchanged in NEC 2017 and I cross-checked the 2017 Chapter 9 PDF for structure). I could NOT confirm from a primary source that PEC 2017 reproduces Table 9 verbatim -- the mm2-based PNS conductor series means PEC may present this differently or may not carry an equivalent table at all. The magnitude of the error (X ~ R at large sizes) is a physics result independent of which code book you use, so the finding stands regardless; but treat the specific XL numbers above as NEC values to be checked against PEC before hard-coding. Note also the sign flip: for conductors 22 mm2 and below the tool is CONSERVATIVE at 0.85 PF, so this is not a uniform under-estimate -- it is specifically a large-conductor / feeder problem.
